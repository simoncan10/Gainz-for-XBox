# Decisions log

Judgement calls made during this run, recorded so later stages/agents know what was chosen and why.

## Stage 1 — Data profiling (2026-08-22)

- **games.csv chosen as the canonical app universe / join base.** Its `app_id` is the only key verified as a true 1:1 primary key that every other table is a subset of (0 orphans in any direction). All join-coverage percentages in `01_profile.json` are expressed as "% of games.csv apps matched", not the reverse, because games.csv is the more useful denominator for a catalogue-wide analysis.

- **Verified the stated games.csv backslash-escape hazard directly, and revised its magnitude.** The task brief cited "~13% of rows" misaligned. Reproducing the failure with Python's plain `csv.reader` (no `escapechar`) actually misaligns 76,334/140,082 rows (54.49%), and positional extraction of `type` after misalignment drops the correct game count from 122,191 to 45,857 (62% loss) — worse than the brief's figure, not better. Reported the measured number, not the brief's number, since the instruction was explicitly to verify and report the real count.

- **Also found that DuckDB's own `read_csv_auto` is unaffected by this hazard** — its sniffer auto-detects `escape='\'` with no configuration. Confirmed by comparing naive-auto-detect vs. explicit `escape='\'` reads: identical row counts and identical `type` distributions. This means the hazard is real and severe for naive Python/pandas pipelines but is not something the data-engineer agent needs to work around if it uses DuckDB's default reader — flagged this distinction explicitly in the profile so downstream stages don't over-correct for a problem DuckDB already avoids.

- **Chose not to attempt combining `nullstr='\N'` with `escape='\'` in a single `read_csv` call** after DuckDB raised a Binder Error confirming the two options are mutually exclusive. Standardized instead on: read with default/auto escape handling, then apply `NULLIF(col, '\N')` + `CAST` in a subsequent SELECT for every affected numeric column. This is the pattern the data-engineer agent should reuse rather than re-discovering the same Binder Error.

- **Reported steamspy_insights.csv's four playtime columns as unusable (constant 0) rather than imputing or estimating a replacement.** Given the target decision is about a subscription business where engagement is central, silently substituting a proxy (e.g. review volume) for missing playtime data was judged out of scope for a profiling stage — that tradeoff belongs to a later analysis stage that can weigh it against the decision it's making.

- **Used exact `count(DISTINCT ...)` for every reported cardinality, not `approx_count_distinct`.** An early exploratory pass used the approximate function for categories.csv and returned 143,470 distinct app_ids vs. the exact 134,393 — a ~7% overstatement that would have inflated the categories join-coverage figure. Approx counts were discarded from all final output; only exact counts appear in the artifacts.

## Stage 2 — Data cleaning / canonical store (2026-08-22)

- **games.csv (`fact_games`, 140,082 rows) chosen as the fact-table grain, keeping demos in with an `is_demo` flag rather than filtering them out.** The profile calls games.csv "the canonical universe" without saying whether demos belong in an investment-relevant catalogue. Dropping 17,891 demo rows (12.8%) silently would be a real analytical choice, not a cleaning step — so they stay, flagged, and the decision of whether to include them is left to whoever runs the analysis.

- **Discovered and fixed a price-currency hazard the stage-1 profile did not catch: `games.csv.price_overview` is 99.1% EUR (of resolvable currencies), not USD.** Reading the raw cents value as USD-cents — the naive interpretation, since nothing in the profile flagged currency — would have understated average price by roughly the EUR/USD gap for essentially the whole priced population. Chose `steamspy_insights.price` (verified genuinely USD, e.g. app_id 10 = 999 cents = Counter-Strike's known $9.99) as the PRIMARY price source, falling back to `price_overview` converted via a static, hand-built, single-point-in-time (~Dec 2024) FX table only for the 819 rows steamspy doesn't cover. The FX table is an approximation, not a live feed — adequate for aggregate USD price statistics (EUR+USD are 99.3% of the FX-converted slice) but explicitly flagged as unsuitable for currency- or time-sensitive pricing claims. A different, equally defensible choice would have been to only use steamspy's native-USD price and treat the other 819 rows as unknown rather than FX-converting them at all; chose the fuller-coverage option and documented the approximation instead.

- **Kept `paid_price_unknown` as a third monetisation state, never coerced to free or to an imputed price.** Combining steamspy + games.csv price sources improved paid-price coverage from the profile's 71.7% (games.csv alone) to 75.5%, but 26,028 nominally-paid titles (18.6% of the whole catalogue, 24.5% of paid titles) still have no price from either source. Any price-based segmentation must explicitly carry this bucket rather than dropping or zeroing it.

- **Dropped steamspy_insights' four playtime columns entirely rather than carrying them forward as-is.** Re-confirmed constant 0 across all 140,077 rows (per profile). Keeping a column that looks populated but silently carries no information is worse than omitting it — an analyst querying `parquet/fact_games.parquet` will not find a playtime column to mistakenly plot, forcing the "no engagement signal in this dataset" limitation to stay visible rather than being one join away from being forgotten.

- **Discovered a category/genre localization hazard NOT in the stage-1 profile, via the mandated 5-record spot check rather than aggregate profiling.** `categories.csv` and `genres.csv` record values in whatever locale the source scrape happened to capture, not uniformly English (795/522,582 category rows and 303/353,339 genre rows are non-ASCII translations of the same ~40-50 canonical types). This silently broke the multiplayer/co-op/indie flags for two of the five spot-checked titles — Dota 2 (Russian categories) and Counter-Strike 2 (Polish categories) — both extremely well-known games that a naive English-string match would have mischaracterized. Chose to make the affected boolean flags resolve to NULL (unknown) rather than false for the 53 apps (0.04% of those with categories) whose category rows are *entirely* non-English, rather than either (a) silently leaving them false, or (b) attempting a full multi-language translation table for all 315 distinct category strings across an unknown set of locales, which was judged disproportionate engineering effort for a hazard this small in aggregate and too easy to get subtly wrong without a verified translation source. Documented explicitly that this fix does not catch Latin-alphabet locales that happen to render in pure ASCII (e.g. Polish "Wieloosobowa") — Counter-Strike 2 itself is a live example of the residual gap, visible directly in the validation spot check. This means has_multiplayer/has_coop/has_controller_support/has_vr/is_indie should be read as a floor, not an exact count.

- **owners_mid computed as the linear midpoint of SteamSpy's bucketed `owners_range`**, e.g. "10,000,000 .. 20,000,000" → 15,000,000. This is a stated approximation, not a real count — flagged explicitly (again) that 83.2% of the catalogue sits in the single bottom bucket (0..20,000 → midpoint 10,000), so owners_mid has essentially no discriminating power for the bulk of the catalogue and should not be treated as a precise ownership estimate.

- **`is_self_published` (developer = publisher) used as a proxy for the requested "first-party vs third-party publisher" flag.** This is a Steam-only dataset with no actual Xbox/Microsoft first-/third-party field — self-published-vs-has-a-distinct-publisher is the closest analogous signal available, not a factual first/third-party classification, and should not be read as one when the analysis reaches conclusions about Xbox specifically.

- **Made every Parquet output byte-identical across reruns by adding an explicit `ORDER BY app_id` (or the natural key) to every final `COPY (SELECT ...)`, despite `preserve_insertion_order = false`.** First full-pipeline rerun test produced different md5sums on 3 of 7 output files with identical row content — traced to `preserve_insertion_order=false` allowing parallel operators to emit rows in a different order each run. `preserve_insertion_order=false` was kept (it's the machine-appropriate setting for intermediate processing) but every terminal `COPY` now sorts its output explicitly, which restores byte-identical Parquet files on rerun (verified via md5sum) without giving up the performance setting.

## Stage 3 — Quantitative analysis (2026-08-22)

- **Used `owners_mid`'s upper tail (mean, top-decile share) as the headline reach statistic, not the median.** Every genre's median `owners_mid` resolves to exactly 10,000 (the bottom-bucket midpoint) because 75-86% of titles in every major genre fall in that single bucket — the median is provably uninformative for this metric. Chose to report mean-based concentration statistics instead (F1), explicitly framed as "hit-concentration," not "typical title performance," rather than reporting a flat, useless median across every segment as if it were a meaningful cut.

- **Defined "primary genre" per app as its globally rarest genre tag**, not its first-listed or an arbitrary pick, when building the developer/publisher out-of-sample expectation model (F4/F5). Genres like Indie/Action/Adventure are near-universal tags (present on 60-80k+ titles) and would otherwise dominate every app's segment assignment, collapsing the genre x price-band segmentation into a few oversized buckets. The rarest-tag heuristic makes the segment assignment more specific and the resulting expectation cell more meaningful, at the cost of being a heuristic rather than Steam's own (nonexistent) "primary genre" field.

- **Set the fit/holdout split at 50/50 by `hash(app_id) % 2`**, and required >=30 titles per (genre, price-band) fit-cell and >=3 (developer) / >=5 (publisher) holdout titles per rollup, before reporting any residual. This is a judgement call trading statistical power (a 70/30 or 80/20 split would give a denser fit model) for a materially larger, more trustworthy holdout evaluation set; chosen because the task explicitly requires the out-of-sample number to be reported honestly, and a thin holdout would make the validation correlation itself noisy and hard to trust.

- **Reported the out-of-sample validation correlation (Pearson r=0.114, Spearman-rank≈0.297) prominently and unflatteringly, rather than only the publisher residual table.** The model's real predictive power is weak — genre and price band alone leave most of the variance in `owners_mid` unexplained. Chose to state this plainly next to every residual-based finding (F4, F5) rather than let a headline-looking "Xbox Game Studios beats expectation by 5.8x" number imply a stronger model than actually exists. This is the direct application of the task's "validate out of sample and report both numbers" instruction.

- **Chose not to build any playtime/engagement proxy from review counts, price, or owner tiers**, despite review count and owners_mid both being available and correlated with popularity. The task brief explicitly forbids presenting an engagement proxy in the language of a measured engagement metric; review volume and reach are reported only as reach/popularity signals, never described as engagement or retention anywhere in `03_findings.md` or `03_findings.json`.

- **Suppressed genre x vintage cells with n<30 in the licensing-inventory query (F6/Q20) but still reported Sports' n=31 combined-across-vintages total explicitly**, rather than omitting Sports from the table entirely. Total silence on a genre directly relevant to the Xbox console context (Steam skews away from sports per Stage 3) would have been a worse outcome than one clearly-flagged, small-n number with its own caveat attached.

## Stage 5 — Investment theses (2026-08-22)

### Candidates generated and killed (the alternatives the board will ask about)

Ten candidates were generated; three survived. Kill reasons, in full:

- **C2 — Acquire or fund a broad indie slate.** Killed on three independent grounds, any one of which would be sufficient. (a) F1's base rate: the top decile of titles takes 78-93% of a genre's audience while 75-86% never leave the bottom owners bucket, so a broad slate is a bet on volume in a market where volume is worthless. (b) The structural record in 04_context.md s4: none of the 2025-26 breakouts (Silksong, Schedule I, Meccha Chameleon) was funded or acquired pre-launch by a platform holder — all broke out organically on Steam first, and Meccha Chameleon went from two developers and two months to 10M copies in 16 days, a tempo no acquisition process can match. (c) It runs directly against the board's own July 2026 decision (xbox-studio-closures-2026-07). Note the brief explicitly permitted making this case anyway if the evidence supported it. It does not — the evidence points the other way, which is why the surviving studio-side thesis (T3) is a recommendation to *decline* studio capital rather than to deploy it.

- **C3 — Increase investment in the already-outperforming Xbox Game Studios and Bethesda.** F4 shows both beating their segment expectation out of sample (5.80x median residual at n=32; 6.23x at n=25). Killed on distinctiveness and on evidentiary weight. Distinctiveness: concentrating on flagship franchises is *already* the stated strategy as of July 2026, so this is a finding that validates a decision already taken, not a recommendation. Retained as confirmatory context inside T3 rather than as a thesis. Weight: the underlying model validates at r=0.114 out of sample, the residuals are computed on n=32/25 Steam PC titles, and F4's own caveat states this is not a console or Game Pass measurement — too thin to justify incremental capital on its own.

- **C4 — Invest in sports back catalogue, the console blind spot.** The most tempting kill in the set. F6 finds only 31 proven-hit sports titles on Steam across all vintages, F8 confirms a ~3.5x shooter-over-sports tag skew, and pc-vs-console-genre-2024 says sports is the top genre on Xbox console — so the genre that matters most on the platform is the one the data can say least about. Killed as a thesis because it would rest on a single secondary Newzoo citation plus a measurement artifact: F6's n=31 is explicitly a Steam PC-skew artifact and is NOT evidence about sports demand in either direction. Recommending a sports investment on that basis would be exactly the population-validity error the brief warns against. Promoted instead to the named first deliverable of T2's measurement capability, where it is the highest-value question to put to console-side data the moment such data exists.

- **C6 — Build a third-party publishing arm rather than owning studios, on F5's distinct-publisher advantage.** Killed on the finding's internal weakness. F5's mean residuals (1.610 distinct-publisher vs 0.904 self-published) look decisive, but the medians are 0.307 vs 0.250 — both far below 1.0, meaning both groups underperform their segment expectation and the mean gap is driven entirely by the tail. Reverse causality is explicitly unexcludable in F5's own caveat (publishers may select projects already trending well rather than causing outperformance), and `is_self_published` is a developer==publisher string proxy, not a real first/third-party field. On top of that it is a softer version of the strategy the board just exited. A thesis resting on a tail-driven gap between two below-expectation medians, measured with a proxy, with the causal arrow unresolved, is not board-grade.

- **C7 — Reprice the Game Pass tiers using F7's price-band evidence.** F7 shows median review volume rising with price up to the $30-60 band. Killed on structural inapplicability: F7 is unit-price data for premium title purchases and is structurally incapable of speaking to *subscription* price elasticity — different decision, different good, different buyer psychology. F7's own caveat states price is confounded with production budget, quality and marketing and that no elasticity can be estimated. The live repricing question (Ultimate $29.99 -> $22.99) is real and important, but this dataset has nothing to say about it, and saying so is better than dressing F7 up as an answer.

- **C9 — Invest in discovery and curation as the Game Pass differentiator.** The best-argued of the kills. F2 establishes real, uncensored ~6.5x supply growth (2,487 titles in 2015 to 16,050 in a 10-month 2024), and F1 establishes that reach concentrates in a top decile — together implying that with 16k+ titles a year arriving, a subscriber's binding constraint is finding something worth playing, not content availability. Good mechanism. Killed because nothing in this dataset shows that Xbox's curation is currently deficient, or that curation quality moves retention — both required links are pure inference, and the second one is unobservable here for the same reason everything about retention is. Folded into T1 as selection discipline (select from the top decile only, ~5% of the available pool) rather than standing as its own capital ask.

- **C10 — Buy day-one exclusivity on a proven third-party franchise.** Rests on silksong-2025-sales: day-one Game Pass inclusion did not fully cannibalise premium sales (7M+ copies in four months). Killed on n=1 against a cost at the $50M top of the observed gp-deal-cost-range. A single non-replicable observation cannot support the most expensive instrument in the deal range. The Silksong evidence is retained inside T1, where it does lighter and more appropriate work: as a counter to the cannibalisation risk on *back-catalogue* titles, whose premium sales curve has already decayed and where the argument is structurally stronger than it is for day-one.

### Judgement calls in constructing the surviving three

- **Ranked T1 (licensing) above T2 (measurement) despite T2 having better arithmetic and higher confidence.** T2's return is pure option value and is entirely contingent on some capital decision existing downstream — measurement with nothing to decide is worth nothing. T1 is the only thesis that generates revenue, and its own first step already contains T2's minimum viable instrumentation ($3M of the $23M). Ranking T2 first would have implied the board should measure before deciding whether to have anything to measure. The dependency is stated explicitly in `ranking_rationale` rather than hidden by the ordering.

- **Kept T2 as a separate thesis rather than collapsing it entirely into T1's first step, despite the overlap.** They are genuinely separable: T2 can be done without T1, and its output feeds the existing licence renewal book, the sports question and the flagship greenlight flow, none of which depend on T1. But T1 at full scale cannot responsibly be done without T2. The overlap and the shared infrastructure are stated in both entries so the board does not double-count the $3M.

- **Made the null option into an explicit recommendation (T3) rather than only assessing it in prose.** The brief put "do nothing and return the capital" genuinely on the table. On the studio side of the ledger it wins outright on the available evidence, and a board that hears only "here are three things to buy" never gets the argument against the proposal that will actually be tabled in FY27. Sizing it as a decision rule with a $50M threshold, rather than as a headline figure, was deliberate: I have no sourced figure for the FY27 studio envelope and declined to fabricate one. The $50M threshold is anchored on the top of the observed per-deal range (gp-deal-cost-range) so that any studio-side proposal exceeding the largest known single content deal must clear the board.

- **Stated plainly that T1 at full scale ($90M/year) does NOT yet clearly beat the null option, and only the staged $23M does.** The break-even hurdle is roughly 1.8-3pp of annual churn improvement and I have no evidence for the magnitude of that delta. Presenting a churn forecast would have made the thesis look stronger and would have been the standard way this analysis fails. Reported the hurdle as a checkable arithmetic bar instead of a projection, and said explicitly that I would not defend a point estimate in the room.

- **Every subscriber-economics number is built from the published tier prices plus a low-confidence third-party subscriber estimate, and is labelled as such.** Microsoft does not disclose subscriber counts (gp-subscribers-estimate-2026 is explicitly low confidence at ~30-34M) and does not publish tier mix. The ~$14 blended ARPU is derived from published prices, not from a published mix; the 60% contribution margin is an unsourced assumption. All three are named in `return.assumptions` rather than carried silently into a headline.

- **Refused to construct any retention or engagement estimate from the data.** The dataset's four playtime columns are constant zero and were dropped in Stage 2; no public source supplies per-genre Game Pass retention data either. Rather than substitute owners or review counts as an engagement proxy — the single most damaging thing this stage could have done for a subscription business — every retention quantity in the theses is either a stated assumption or a break-even hurdle to be measured later, and `what_this_analysis_cannot_tell_the_board` names this first.

- **Ran a self-adversarial pass in place of the red-team-critic round, and recorded it as a weaker substitute rather than an equivalent.** No critique had arrived at drafting time and no subagent-spawning tool is available in this stage's environment (the exposed task tools are todo-list only). Nine objections were generated and answered under the mandated Concede/Bound/Rebut discipline, producing six substantive revisions to 05_theses.json (contribution-margin falsifier, catalogue-overlap screen, F4 demoted in T3, breakout claim narrowed to a tempo argument, sports promoted in T2, materiality objection answered at top level). 05_responses.md states explicitly that a self-generated critique is systematically weaker than an independent one and leaves Round 2 open for the critic.
